# Facilitation, method combinations, evidence and history

## Running SCAMPER with a group

**Brainwrite first, discuss second.** Diehl and Stroebe (1987) found that interactive "shout-out" brainstorming groups produce fewer ideas than the same number of people working alone, mainly because people block each other while waiting their turn. So for each letter, people write silently first (sticky notes, or 6-3-5 brainwriting), then share and build on each other's ideas.

**Roles.** A facilitator does four things:
- keeps everyone on one letter at a time;
- enforces deferred judgment;
- pushes for "How else?";
- watches the clock.

A diverse group (different departments, customers, outsiders) helps most with Adapt and Put to other uses.

**Suggested agenda** (60–90 min):

| Time | Step |
|---|---|
| 5 min | Frame: the subject, the "How might we…?", the success criterion, the constraints |
| 5–10 min | Break the subject into 4–6 attributes on the wall |
| 35–50 min | One letter at a time, 5–7 min each: 2 min silent writing per person, then share and cluster. Spend longer on E and R |
| 10 min | Silent dot vote, then How–Now–Wow or NUF on the top ideas |
| 10 min | Hybridize the top ideas; agree owners and the cheapest tests |

The timings are practitioner convention, not research findings. Adjust them.

**Prompt cards.** When asked to prepare a workshop, produce one card per letter. Each card has:
- the operator name;
- 4–6 trigger questions tailored to the subject;
- one worked example from a field *outside* the team's own.

**Solo use.** SCAMPER works well alone. It was designed as a personal checklist. Write everything down, then do a forced second round of "How else? What else?".

## Combining SCAMPER with other methods
- **Mind map or attribute listing**, then SCAMPER each branch.
- **Customer journey map**, then SCAMPER the most painful steps.
- **5 Whys or fishbone** to find the root cause, then SCAMPER the fix.
- **Morphological box (Zwicky box).** Use SCAMPER to fill each column with options, then combine across columns.
- **TRIZ** for the hard technical contradiction, then SCAMPER to polish the solution. Several TRIZ principles echo SCAMPER: segmentation is close to Minify (split it), inversion to Reverse, merging to Combine.
- **Reverse brainstorming** ("how would we make it worse?") is an extended form of R.
- **SCAMPER, then Six Thinking Hats or PMI** for evaluation.

## Evidence and limitations: be honest about these
- **What the evidence supports.** Many small classroom studies report gains on Torrance-style creativity tests after SCAMPER training, for example a kindergarten study in Turkey (2019) and creative-writing studies with 9th graders. These gains are mostly in **fluency** (the number of ideas). **Originality** improves least consistently. Many of the studies are small or have weak controls.
- **Comparisons with other methods.** In engineering-design studies:
  - SCAMPER ranked above free brainstorming and "no method", but below functional analysis.
  - SCAMPER produced more *feasible* ideas, while brainstorming sometimes produced the single most *novel* one.
  - A 2020 game-ideation study found no significant differences between SCAMPER, a card kit and free brainstorming.
- **Creativity training in general.** A 2024 meta-analysis (Sio and Lortie-Forgues, *Psychological Bulletin*, 169 studies) found an unadjusted effect of about 0.5 SD. After adjusting for strong publication bias, the effect shrinks to about 0.3 SD.
- **Practical implication.** Treat SCAMPER as a dependable tool for *coverage and volume*, not a guarantee of breakthroughs. Quality comes from breaking the subject into attributes, pushing for bold ideas, reaching to distant analogies, and selecting carefully.
- **Known limitations:**
  - It anchors on the existing artifact. Design-fixation research (Jansson and Smith, 1991) shows starting examples bias people to copy their features, so it tends toward incremental, "new to us" change.
  - Used mechanically, it gives cosmetic variants.
  - It has no built-in way to define the problem or evaluate ideas.
  - It is weak on technical contradictions.

## History and variants
- **Alex F. Osborn**, an advertising executive at BBDO. *Applied Imagination* (1953; revised 1957 and 1963) introduced brainstorming and a checklist of roughly 70 idea-spurring questions. The checklist had nine headings: Put to other uses, Adapt, Modify, Magnify, Minify, Substitute, Rearrange, Reverse, Combine. Precise counts like "73" or "75" are widely repeated but unverified.
- **Bob Eberle**, an educator. *SCAMPER: Games for Imagination Development* (1971) turned the checklist into the mnemonic, as imagination games for children. He followed with "Developing Imagination Through SCAMPER" in the *Journal of Creative Behavior* (1972).
- **Michael Michalko**. *Thinkertoys* (1991; 2nd ed. 2006) popularized SCAMPER for business. His procedure:
  1. Isolate the subject.
  2. Ask the questions of each part of it.
  3. Push every answer with "How else? What else?".
  4. Evaluate.
- **Variants:**
  - R as Reverse, Rearrange, or both. "SCAMPERR" uses both.
  - "SCAMMPERR" splits both M (Magnify and Minify) and R (Rearrange and Reverse).
  - A as Adjust, and P as Purpose, appear in some versions.
  - "SCRAMPER" is an informal misspelling.
  - This skill uses the seven letters, with M as a triad and R as a pair, which recovers all nine of Osborn's headings.
