# A — Adapt

**Definition.** Borrow a solution that already works somewhere else, such as another industry, nature, history, a different medium or a competitor, and fit it to this problem.

**The move.** This is analogical transfer. Map the *underlying mechanism* from a source domain onto your problem. Copying only the surface look is a weak adaptation. Distant sources give more original results than near ones.

## Trigger questions
- What else is like this? (Osborn)
- What other idea does this suggest? (Osborn)
- Does the past offer a parallel? (Osborn)
- What could I copy? Whom could I emulate? (Osborn)
- Who has solved a structurally similar problem in a completely different industry?
- How does nature solve this?
- How would Disney, IKEA, a hospital emergency room, a Formula 1 pit crew, a video game, or a museum do it?
- Which successful format from another medium could be retooled for this?
- Which pattern from sport, games, the military or religion applies?
- Could the target group change, borrowing how *their* world works?
- What did people do about this 100 years ago?
- What works for our audience in a completely different part of their life?

When you name a source, also name the mechanism you are borrowing from it, not only the source.

## By domain
- **Content / marketing:**
  - Turn a top-performing blog post into a video.
  - Borrow formats from elsewhere: a movie trailer, a recipe, software "patch notes", a sports draft, a weather forecast, a court case, a museum audio guide.
  - Borrow a competitor's best-performing *structure* but invert the angle.
- **Writing:**
  - Retell a myth or classic. *Clueless* adapts *Emma*.
  - Borrow structure from another medium: an epistolary novel, an oral history, a podcast transcript, a choose-your-own-adventure.
  - Apply a screenwriting beat sheet to an essay.
  - Write a product page as a field guide.
- **Design / UI:**
  - Borrow checkout patterns from other industries.
  - Borrow game mechanics such as progress, streaks and unlocks.
  - Use editorial magazine layouts for dashboards, airport wayfinding for navigation, or a recipe-card structure for help docs.
- **Business / process:**
  - Formula 1 pit stops and aviation handovers were applied to surgery-to-ICU handovers at Great Ormond Street Hospital. The study reported fewer handover errors and shorter handovers (Catchpole et al. 2007).
  - American supermarket restocking inspired Toyota's kanban pull system, which Ohno cited.
  - Aviation checklists have been applied to surgery.

## Pitfalls
- Surface copying, taking the look without the mechanism.
- Not checking whether the source's conditions hold in the new context.
- Only adapting from close competitors, which gives me-too output.

## Verified or well-documented examples
- **Shinkansen 500 Series (1997).** Engineer Eiji Nakatsu modelled the nose on the kingfisher's beak to reduce the "tunnel boom" that happens when the train leaves a tunnel.
- **Velcro.** George de Mestral adapted the hooks of burdock burrs.
- **Ford's moving assembly line.** Ford's own account credits the overhead trolleys Chicago meatpackers used. Historians describe a more gradual evolution, so attribute it as "Ford's account".
- **Dyson.** Adapted industrial cyclone separators for vacuum cleaners, according to Dyson's own account.
