# E — Eliminate

**Definition.** Remove parts, features, steps, rules, costs, words or assumptions. Simplify, streamline, and reduce it to the core.

**The move.** Subtraction. Research on "subtraction neglect" (Adams et al., *Nature*, 2021) suggests people systematically overlook subtractive changes and default to adding. That is why Eliminate deserves extra effort. It often produces the most distinctive and cheapest ideas in the whole session.

## Trigger questions
- What could be removed without losing the function?
- What isn't necessary? (Michalko)
- What could be omitted, streamlined or understated? (Osborn)
- What would happen if this component simply weren't there?
- Which step do customers hate most? Can it go entirely?
- Which rule exists only because of history?
- What could the customer skip, or do themselves?
- What if there were no physical product, no meeting, no form, no login?
- What is the one thing this must do? What happens to everything else?
- Which feature or section do fewer than 10% of people use?
- What if we halved the budget, the time, the word count or the number of options?
- What would the version with no ___ look like (no images, no intro, no discount, no logo)?

## By domain
- **Content / marketing:**
  - Remove the intro, the gate, the jargon, the stock photos, the second CTA.
  - Strip the campaign down to one message.
  - Cut the funnel step with the most drop-off.
  - Drop a channel and go deeper on one.
- **Writing:**
  - Cut the throat-clearing first paragraph; the start is often paragraph two.
  - Remove a character, a subplot, a flashback.
  - Delete adverbs and qualifiers.
  - Remove the narrator's explanation and trust the reader.
  - Kill the subhead that repeats the headline.
- **Design / UI:**
  - Remove checkout steps, required fields, decorative chrome, a navigation level, a confirmation dialog.
  - Test the page with no images, or with one font and one colour.
- **Business / process:**
  - Remove a sign-off, a report nobody reads, a policy relic, a middleman, a meeting.
  - Use Lean's waste categories as a checklist: waiting, over-processing, rework, motion, inventory, transport, over-production.

## Pitfalls
- Cutting something users value but never mention, such as tactile feel, a feeling of safety, or ritual.
- Removing the thing that differentiates you.
- Removing so much that the item no longer signals its category, and people don't recognize it.
- Usability-test eliminations in UI. Unconventional removals can confuse people.

## Verified or well-documented examples
- **IKEA flat-pack.** In IKEA's origin story, Gillis Lundgren removed a table's legs in 1956 so it would fit in a car. Note that ready-to-assemble furniture existed earlier.
- **Dyson.** Eliminated the vacuum bag.
- **Netflix's DVD subscription.** Eliminated late fees.
- **McDonald's.** The McDonald brothers' 1948 Speedee Service System eliminated carhops and most of the menu. It was not Ray Kroc's invention.
